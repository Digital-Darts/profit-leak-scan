# Glossary

How to explain each number to a store owner the first time it appears in the report. Use these phrasings. They are written for someone who runs a store, not someone who runs ad accounts.

Never use a term without explaining it once. Never explain it twice.

---

**ROAS.** Revenue divided by ad spend, as Google counts it. A 4x ROAS means Google believes it returned $4 of sales for every $1 spent. It says nothing about whether you made money. An account with cost of goods in Merchant Center and cart data reporting switched on can show gross profit as well, which is better, but that still stops before outbound shipping, payment fees and returns.

**Break-even ROAS.** The ROAS you need just to stand still, worked out from your margin. If 40 cents of every dollar is left after product cost, shipping and fees, you need 2.5x before the ads have paid for themselves. Anything below that is costing you money to run, no matter how good the ROAS looks next to someone else's.

**POAS, profit on ad spend.** The same idea as ROAS but using profit instead of revenue. It answers the question ROAS cannot: did this campaign put money in the bank. A campaign at 6x ROAS selling a low-margin product can sit below a campaign at 3x selling a high-margin one.

**Contribution margin.** The share of each sale left after product cost, shipping and payment fees, before any ad spend. If a $100 order leaves you $40, your contribution margin is 40%. Every break-even figure in this report is worked out from it.

**Contribution.** What is left after product cost, shipping, payment fees and the ad spend. This is the number that pays your wages, your rent and everything else. If contribution is negative, the campaign is being funded by the rest of the business.

**Ex GST.** A figure with the GST taken out. GST goes to the tax office, so it is not part of what the store earns. Every profit figure in this report is ex GST, even when Google's numbers include it. For a UK store, read VAT. In the US, sales tax is added at checkout, so prices are already ex tax.

**Brand leak.** The share of your ad spend that went to people searching for your own name. They already knew about you. Most of them were coming anyway, so a lot of that spend buys a sale you already had.

**Incremental.** A sale that only happened because of the ad. The opposite is a sale you would have got anyway and paid for twice. Most of the difference between what an ad platform reports and what your bank shows is this.

**New customer ROAS.** The same calculation, counting only first-time buyers. If total ROAS is holding up while new customer ROAS falls, the account is growing today by spending down the customers you already had.

**Attribution.** Which ad gets credit for a sale. Every platform claims the ones it touched, so if you add up what Google, Meta and your email tool each claim, you will have sold more than the store actually made.

**Conversion value.** The dollar figure Google records against a sale. It should be what the customer paid for the products. If it includes GST and delivery, every ROAS in the account is inflated by the same amount. This scan takes the GST out before doing any maths. If the account bids higher for new customers, Google also adds the new-customer value you set to this figure. The Original conv. value column shows it without the addition, and that is the one this scan uses when you supply it.

**Conversion value rule.** A setting that tells Google to multiply or add to the value of some sales, for example sales in one city or on one device. It changes what Google reports and bids on. It does not change what the customer paid.

**Customer acquisition.** A Google Ads setting that changes how a campaign bids on people who have never bought from you. "Bid higher for new customers" bids more for them and adds a value you choose to each of their sales in Google's reports. "Only bid for new customers" stops the campaign showing to existing customers.

**Value per sale.** What Google records for an average sale, set beside your average order in Shopify. When the two agree, the tag is recording real order values.

**Refunded share.** The part of your order value that was later refunded. Google records a sale when the order is placed and does not take it back, so its figures run higher than what you kept by about this share.

**Searches Google does not list.** Google leaves low-volume searches out of the search terms report. The money spent on them still appears as one total, so the report can say how much it cannot see.

**Conversion lag.** The time between someone clicking an ad and buying. Google counts the sale against the day of the click, so the most recent days in any report look worse than they will once the late sales arrive.

**Search term.** What someone actually typed into Google. Different from a keyword, which is what you told Google you wanted to show up for. The gap between the two is where wasted spend lives.

**Negative keyword.** A word or phrase that stops your ad showing. The cheapest fix in most accounts.

**Pattern.** A run of one, two or three words that appears across many different searches. Useful because you can judge a pattern, and block it, once, instead of a hundred individual searches one at a time.

**Performance Max.** Google's campaign type that runs across Search, Shopping, YouTube, Gmail, Discover and Display in one budget, with less to see and control than any other campaign type. It works, and the cost of it working is that you see less of what it is doing. It will bid on your brand name unless you stop it.

**Standard Shopping.** The older Shopping campaign, where you can see which products and searches spend your money. Less automated, more visible.

**Demand Gen.** Google's campaign type for reaching people before they search, across YouTube, Shorts, Discover, Gmail, Maps and the Display Network. It creates demand rather than harvesting it.

**Ad Rank.** Google's score for deciding which ad shows. It combines your bid with how well the ad is expected to do for that search. When two of your own campaigns could show for the same search, the one with the higher Ad Rank is the one that shows.

**Product feed.** The file Google reads to decide when your products show, against whom, and at what price. For most stores it was created on launch day by clicking "connect" in the Google & YouTube app and has not been looked at since.

**Cost per item.** The field in Shopify where you record what a product costs you. Most stores leave it blank, which is why most stores cannot see profit on anything.

**Merchant Center.** Where your product feed lives on Google's side. Separate from Google Ads, and the place feed problems show up first.

**Geo holdout.** The reliable way to find out whether a channel is actually working. You turn it off in a few regions whose sales usually track the rest of the store, leave it on everywhere else, and compare. It is the only method here that measures your store rather than estimating from someone else's.
